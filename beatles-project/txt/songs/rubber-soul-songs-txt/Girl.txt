Song Facts

• “Girl” is primarily written by John, with contributions from Paul.

• The Beatles recorded the song during the final recording session for <em>Rubber Soul</em>, as they worked to meet the deadline for the album’s pre-Christmas release.

• The song’s “Zorba-like” section was written by Paul, inspired by a band playing at the hotel during his 1963 holiday in Greece with Ringo: “I was very impressed with another culture’s approach because it was slightly different from what we did. We just did it on acoustic guitars instead of bouzoukis”.

• In Barry Miles’ book <em>Many Years from Now</em>, Paul recalled coming up with the lines “Was she told when she was young that pain would lead to pleasure” and “That a man must break his back to earn his day of leisure”. However, in a 1970 interview with <em>Rolling Stone</em>, John said he wrote these lines as a comment on Christianity:

“I was just talking about Christianity [...] and that was sort of the Catholic Christian concept — to be tortured and then it’ll be all right, which seems to be a bit true but not in their concept of it. But I didn’t believe in that, that you have to be tortured to attain anything, it just so happens that you were”.

• The song was released as a single in Italy and France in 1966, while in Spain it appeared as the title track of an EP alongside three other tracks from <em>Rubber Soul</em>.

• In the UK, a cover version by St. Louis Union was released as the group’s debut single, reaching number 11 on the UK Singles Chart in February 1966.

• In his final interview in 1980, John described his solo song “Woman” as “the grown-up version of ‘Girl’”.

Words from John and Paul

‘Girl’ is real. There is no such thing as the girl; she was a dream, but the words are all right. It wasn’t just a song, and it was about that girl – that turned out to be Yoko, in the end – the one that a lot of us were looking for.

— John Lennon (1970), <em>The Beatles Anthology</em>, 2000

It was John’s original idea, but it was very much co-written. I remember writing ‘the pain and pleasure’ and ‘a man must break his back’. It was all very ‘Working on the Chain Gang’.

My main memory is that John wanted to hear the breathing, wanted it to be very intimate, so George Martin put a special compressor on the voice, then John dubbed it.

It was always amusing to see if we could get a naughty word on the record: ‘fish and finger pie’ [on ‘Penny Lane’], ‘prick teaser’ [on ‘Day Tripper’], ‘tit tit tit tit’ [on ‘Girl’]. The Beach Boys had a song out where they’d done ‘la la la la’ [on ‘You‘re So Good To Me’], and we loved the innocence of that and wanted to copy it, but not use the same phrase. So we were looking around for another phrase, so it was ‘dit dit dit dit’, which we decided to change in our waggishness to ‘tit tit tit tit’, which is virtually indistinguishable from ‘dit dit dit dit’. And it gave us a laugh. It was to get some light relief in the middle of this real big career that we were forging.

If we could put in something that was a little bit subversive, then we would. George Martin might say, ‘Was that ‘dit dit’ or ‘tit tit’ you were singing?‘ ‘Oh, ‘dit dit’, George, but it does sound a bit like that, doesn’t it?’ Then we’d get in the car and break down laughing.
— Paul McCartney, <em>Many Years from Now</em>, 1997
